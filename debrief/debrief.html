(0,0)=52 → (0,1)=58 → (1,1)=64 → (2,1)=70: 3 steps. At (0,0), right and down tie at 58, so right wins because it comes first.

2. Gennai: why did only some fate scrolls reach the summit? (simulated annealing)

A downhill move of size Δ < 0 is accepted with probability eΔ/T, and Tn = T0·0.97n. Escaping the false peak means chaining several −5 to −7 steps downhill to reach the valley floor (53, 17 below the peak). That is only likely while T is large compared with those steps.

The run is decided early. With T₀ = 20, the chance of taking a −7 step falls below 1% at iteration 85. After that the walk is effectively hill climbing: uphill and level moves are always taken, so it climbs to the top of whichever hill it is on. Success therefore depends on which basin the walker is in when it freezes. The seed only decides the random path through those first ~85 iterations.

With T₀ = 20 that happens for 8 of the 20 seeds (SA_SUCCESS_1_TO_20 = 8). The other 12 freeze on the left hill and end at (2,1) = 70. In every T₀ = 20 run the best value equals the final value, so no run touched the summit and then lost it.

Starting heat 40

Doubling T₀ shifts the whole schedule later by ln 2 / ln(1/0.97) ≈ 23 iterations. The 1% freeze point moves from iteration 85 to 108. Each run therefore makes more risky moves: 20–32 downhill moves accepted, against 12–20 at T₀ = 20. Successes rise from 8/20 to 12/20.

  • Seeds 4, 8, 10, 13 and 19 now succeed. Only seed 6 fails where it used to succeed.
  • Seed 6 (best 74) and seed 15 (best 81) crossed onto the right ridge but wandered back while the walk was still hot, so they ended at 70. Extra heat buys exploration but also lets a walker leave good ground.
  • Seeds reach 95 later (first visit at iteration 20–108, against 20–73) and keep leaving it for longer (last exit up to iteration 106), because the walk stays restless for longer.

Heat and cooling together set how long the "hot" phase lasts compared with the 200-iteration budget. Extra sweep, successes out of 20:

Seed by seed, the pouches differ in exactly four seeds. The GA keeps Flash tags and Twin blades (load 19, volume 12, use 29). The optimum packs Smoke bombs and Medical kit instead (load 20, volume 13, use 36). The swap gains 7 usefulness for 1 more load and 1 more volume, which fills both limits exactly.

Why the seeds missed it:

  • 234 is a local optimum in bit space. Each of the 15 one-bit changes either breaks a limit (fitness 0) or scores 196–226. Brute force shows that 241 is the only feasible pouch better than 234, and it is exactly four flips away. Mutation flips each bit with p = 1/15, so a child gets precisely those four flips with probability (1/15)4(14/15)11 ≈ 9×10−6. Over the ~630 children bred after generation 15, that is under 1%.
  • The fitness cliff blocks gradual paths. Adding Smoke bombs or the Medical kit first overflows a limit and scores 0. So every intermediate step toward 241 is worthless and selection discards it.
  • Premature convergence removed the missing building blocks. The GA reached 234 at generation 15 (GA_FIRST_GEN) and never improved. Average pairwise Hamming distance fell from 7.38 (gen 0) to 2.26 (gen 50). In the final population, bits 5 and 10 are set in 18 and 19 of the 20 chromosomes, but bits 2 and 12 (the ones 241 needs) appear in only 1 each. Crossover cannot assemble genes the population no longer carries. A population of 20 with two elites and size-3 tournaments applies strong selection pressure, so a single good pouch quickly takes over.

One fix: a repair operator (plus a little more mutation). After mutation, if a child breaks a limit, unpack its least valuable tool (lowest usefulness per unit of scaled load + volume) until it fits. This removes the cliff: an "add Medical kit" mutation becomes "add Medical kit, drop Flash tags", which is a feasible step toward 241. Middle-first does exactly what it promises, where the best move really is in the middle. Vesper's refutation of every losing X move is O2, so with column 2 first each losing move is refuted in 2 nodes. That saves 2,274 nodes against the 01234 order (1,592 + 330 + 103 + 257 − 8).

But most of the work (75% of AB1's nodes) is inside the one move that matters, X2, and there middle-first is worse: 8,956 nodes against 6,781 (+2,175). After X2 the "middle" is nearly gone. Columns 1 and 3 have one empty cell each and column 2 has two, and the main line opens O0 X0 O1. Putting the middle columns first there does not bring strong replies forward, and the measured cost goes up because weaker moves are tried before the cut-offs arrive. The gain at the root and the loss inside X2 almost cancel: 2,274 − 2,175 = 99.

Across all 120 column orders, full alpha-beta ranges from 2,284 to 29,284 nodes (median 8,188). Middle-first ranks 65th of 120 and 01234 ranks 66th. The best fixed order (13240) needs 2,284. Move ordering matters a great deal, but a single "middle first" rule ignores where this position's strong moves actually are.

Exact values of the root's children: A = −15, B = 9, C = −4. Left to right reads 39 leaves (A 20/29, B 16/19, C 3/12). Right to left reads only 23 (C 10/12, B 8/19, A 5/29), for the same value 9. Pruning needs good bounds early, and the right-hand side of this tree supplies them:

  • A better opening bound at the root. Left to right starts with A, the worst child (−15), so B is searched with α = −15, a weak bound. Right to left starts with C = −4, so α = −4 is already tighter before B.
  • Inside B, the decisive child comes first. B's value is set by B2 (9). Right to left reaches B2 before B1, so β = 9 is known when B1 starts. B1d = 26 ≥ 9 cuts B1c, B1b and B1a at once (only 2 of B1's 11 leaves read). Left to right read all 11 of B1's leaves before B2 gave anything useful.
  • A is dismissed cheaply. When right to left reaches A, α = 9. Each grandchild of A3 is cut after its first leaf (L29 = −28, L27 = 4, L23 = −29 after L24, L22 = 7, all ≤ 9), so A3 = 7 ≤ 9 and A2 and A1 are never touched: 19 leaves skipped. Left to right had to search A first with no bound at all.

This is the same lesson as Stone Drop: alpha-beta's saving comes from meeting each node's strongest reply first. In this tree, the strong values (C's −4, B2's 9) sit on the right, so right-to-left is the near-ideal order. In Stone Drop, a fixed rule like "middle first" only helps where the strong moves really are in the middle.
